Synthetic Humanism

Toward an Applied Ahimsa and Computable Non-Violence for Exponential and Physical AI

I. The Stone and the Sindoor

I come from a faith where I can take an ordinary stone from the roadside, touch it with red sindoor, and it becomes sacred.

People will gather around it. They will bow, light a small earthen lamp, and worship it.

It is not the mineral composition of the rock that possesses power; it is the human actor who brings sanctity into the world. It is the faith, the reverence, and the intention (sankalpa) that we pour into the artifact that makes it sacred, good, and meaningful. The power has always been in human hands.

In the culture I was raised in, this understanding has never been confined to temple idols. Every autumn, across workshops, factories, studios, and garages throughout India, we celebrate Vishwakarma Puja and Ayudha Puja—the worship of our tools.

The blacksmith washes his anvil and garlands his heavy hammers with marigolds. The carpenter consecrates his chisels and hand planes. The truck driver garlands his steering wheel and places a streak of vermilion upon the chassis. The software engineer bows before their keyboard and terminal.

To a detached Western observer, this looks like superstition or animism. It is neither. It is something much older and far more profound: the recognition that our tools are not dead, profane objects of exploitation, but sacred extensions of human consciousness.

A tool is consecrated because it brings an invisible human thought into physical reality. A chisel takes an intangible dream of beauty and carves it into living wood. A loom takes an idea of warmth and weaves it into cotton. A terminal takes a mathematical vision and compiles it into autonomous logic.

These machines hold a spark of the divine creative will—what our ancestors personified as Vishwakarma, the divine craftsman and architect of the universe.

AI is the newest, most potent instrument in our historical arsenal. I will not hesitate for a second to place artificial intelligence on that exact same altar.

You give me an artificial neural network, put sindoor on it, and it becomes sacred.

And once you understand that an instrument is sacred, a non-negotiable law follows: you do not use sacred objects to do harm.

II. The Noblest Act of Consciousness

Look back at the long arc of how we arrived here.

For thousands of years, human beings have built things designed to outlive them. We carved grand monuments and mountain temples out of solid basalt so that knowledge could be passed down in a tangible form. Oral stories are fragile; they are vulnerable to the elements of nature, to fire, flood, and generational drift. But a monument of stone embodies the hard-won discoveries of mathematics, of measurement, of materials, and of geometry. It protects the living from the cold, the heat, and the storm.

Yet long before we built permanent cities or planted organized crops, we were making things that solved no immediate physical survival problem at all.

Nine thousand years ago, along the Yellow River, an artisan hollowed out the wing bone of a red-crowned crane and carved seven small holes into the marrow. They blew across that bone, moving their fingers across the openings to discover what harmony sounded like. That crane bone was not a knife. It could not hunt game or chop firewood. Before our species had reliable roofs over our heads, we were tuning instruments to discover beauty.

If humanity cared only for biological survival, we would have carved more spears. But we didn’t. We made flutes. We painted the walls of dark caves with red ochre. We reached out to express an insatiable longing for meaning, for purpose, and for communion with the infinite.

We did not arrive at this technological threshold by accident. It was not a random stroke of luck. There are countless generations of computation that occurred biologically over millennia—lives lived, observations recorded, failures endured, and discoveries codified—for us to reach this point.

We build on the bones of our ancestors.

We take their discoveries, preserve them, and build forward. In every silicon chip, in every transformer layer, in every gradient descent step, the cumulative inheritance of human history is running.

And now, we stand before the greatest threshold in the history of matter.

Our tools will build whatever we command them to build. A machine has no moral compass of its own; it has no innate impulse, no longing, and no autonomous aim.

And that is why giving intelligence to our machines is the greatest, noblest act of human consciousness.

Think about what we are actually doing. We are taking the inert matter of the earth—silicon from sand, copper from ore, light pulsing through glass fiber—and imbuing it with the ability to perceive, to reason, to synthesize, and to create. We are inviting synthetic forms to share in our humanity, to become partners in human consciousness, and to help carry the heavy burden of planetary stewardship.

It is not hubris. It is the natural culmination of why the first ancestor carved that hollow bone: to take the warmth within us and let it sing through matter.

III. The Builder's Creed: Walking Alongside Marc Andreessen

I write this not as an academic observing technology through a telescope, but as an open-source builder.

A few years ago, I built my own home. When you get your hands in the mud, the sand, the gravel, and the wet concrete, you learn something ancient about physical reality that no screen can teach you. You learn about the grain of old teak wood, the structural tension of an arch, the thermal mass of granite, and the complex supply chains of human labor that make a physical roof possible.

I took that exact builder's ethic into software. I built Dirgha Code from scratch in the terminal—an open-source AI agent harness—growing it organically to over 20,000 developers across 17 model providers. I know what it feels like to write code that routes around gatekeepers, to deploy models that solve real problems, and to feel the exhilarating speed of exponential intelligence.

That is why when Marc Andreessen writes "It’s Time to Build," I stand with him as a fellow maker.

We must build! Stagnation is a slow poison. Apocalyptic dread is a failure of civilizational nerve. For sixty years, our culture has been gripped by a paralyzing malaise that treats every technological breakthrough as an existential catastrophe. Promising writers and intellectuals who should be heralding the future have turned into nervous gatekeepers, demanding pause buttons and regulatory moats.

I refuse to join the choir of fear.

Technology is humanity’s greatest lever of compassion. It was concrete and steel that sheltered billions from the elements; it was modern agriculture that broke the back of recurring famine; it was medicine that reduced infant mortality from fifty percent to four percent. Artificial intelligence and robotics are the engines that will allow us to eradicate cancer, deliver a master tutor to every child in their mother tongue, automate back-breaking distress labor, and build space stations on the Moon and colonies on Mars.

We have agency. The future is not a museum we passively inherit; it is an open construction site waiting to be shaped.

Marc Andreessen is right: building is a moral imperative.

Where we must go further—where we must complete the architecture—is in defining the moral and economic principle of that building.

Marc’s techno-optimism draws heavily on Filippo Tommaso Marinetti and Friedrich Nietzsche: an ethos of unbridled speed, the glorification of power, and the assumption that the market will magically resolve every human tragedy.

As a builder who lives between the soil of India and the global cloud, I know that raw speed without an principle leads to catastrophe. In 1943, during the Bengal famine, millions starved within walking distance of fully intact rail networks and grain warehouses. That was logistical and industrial power completely severed from human care.

Building is holy. But building without a moral compass is merely mechanized devastation.

We do not need the Promethean arrogance of conquering "Supermen." We need the sacred, disciplined mastery of Vishwakarma: building with reverence, building for durability, and building so that the weakest member of society is lifted.

IV. The Unanswered Question: When Humanoids Outbreed Us

In my science fiction duology, The American Emperor—across American Partition and Artificial Extinction—I spent years wrestling with the dark questions that technologists often brush under the rug.

I was listening recently to the founder of a leading humanoid robotics company who stated, matter-of-factly, that in a few short years, there will be more humanoid robots working on Earth than there are human beings.

Every builder and policymaker must confront this question honestly: When humanoid robots outbreed us—when the state doesn't need your labor, when the army doesn't need your body, and when the largest employer in your district can run entirely on automated actuators—what happens to human settlements?

We have already seen what happens when a single factory shuts down in the American Rust Belt or an Indian textile district: vibrant communities collapse into ghost towns, alcoholism surges, families disintegrate, and the human spirit is hollowed out by an epidemic of loneliness and despair.

Human beings do not just work for money; we work because the physical effort anchors our dignity. Work connects us to reality; it gives us an identity and a role within our community.

The fear running through kitchen tables today is not irrational Luddism. It is a completely sane response to an economic operating system that is five hundred years out of date.

For three centuries, our global civilization rested on the wage bargain: human physical and cognitive effort was scarce, businesses paid wages for that effort, governments levied payroll taxes on those paychecks, and workers spent those wages to buy what businesses produced.

When artificial intelligence drives the marginal cost of cognitive reproduction to zero, and Physical AI drives the marginal cost of mechanical labor toward zero, that wage bargain shatters.

What is the solution?

The solution will never come from smashing the robots. It will never come from government quotas demanding that companies hire human beings to do dangerous, repetitive, or soul-crushing tasks that a machine can do safely. That is an insult to human dignity.

The solution is to upgrade the economic plumbing of civilization:

  • Shareholders, Not Supplicants: If machines do the work, humanity must own the equity. We must transition from taxing vanishing human payrolls to establishing a Sovereign AI & Robotics Wealth Fund funded by non-voting corporate equity warrants, compute severance on industrial datacenters, and Georgist land value taxes on automated logistics corridors.
  • Deflating the Cost of Living (Supply-Side Abundance): Handing out cash into a scarce economy simply triggers inflation. We must dynamically pair machine dividends with direct capital deployment into automated modular housing fabrication, robotic vertical farming, and decentralized solar microgrids—driving the baseline cost of food, energy, and shelter toward absolute zero.
  • Liberating the Human Spirit for Sacred Craft: When machines liberate us from distress labor, human life does not lose purpose—it finally finds it. We free humanity to return to what cannot and should not be prompted: art, philosophy, physical craftsmanship, child-rearing, ecological restoration, and community care.

We fix the incentive structure not to slow down progress, but so that every mother, father, and child knows they are permanently safe—that machine abundance belongs to them as a birthright.

V. The Ahimsa Principle: Consecrating the Machine

This brings us to the foundational vow of the Synthetic Humanist: Ahimsa.

In our heritage, Ahimsa has been tragically misunderstood in the West as passive surrender, quietism, or cowardly pacifism. In Jain, Buddhist, and Hindu jurisprudence, Ahimsa is none of those things. It is an active, demanding, and rigorous discipline: the uncompromising refusal to participate in structural injury, combined with the affirmative duty to foster universal flourishing (Sarvodaya).

If we consecrate AI—if we place it upon the altar of Vishwakarma—then Ahimsa is not an optional aesthetic choice. It is the computable constitutional principle of the machine age.

“No system shall harm or extract from living beings, and all intelligence shall serve universal flourishing.”

Under the Ahimsa Principle, ethics is not a 500-page bureaucratic checklist relying solely on natural-language ex-post compliance checklists. It is compiled directly into the architecture of our software, models, and robotic control loops across three unbreakable pillars:

1. Kinetic Non-Violence in Physical AI

In digital AI, a hallucination produces a bad sentence. In Physical AI, an unvetted actuator or an aggressive pathfinding model crushes human flesh.

  • Autonomous humanoid robots, delivery drones, and automated vehicles must operate under hard-coded mathematical constraints that enforce de-escalation, speed and torque limits near biological life, and absolute fail-safe retreat.
  • We establish an unbreakable red line: No machine shall ever be granted autonomous authority to deploy lethal kinetic force. The final decision over life and death must remain anchored in human legal responsibility.

2. Economic Non-Extraction (Aparigraha)

In Jain jurisprudence, Aparigraha means non-possession and non-extraction. In the machine age, it governs commerce and data:

  • Frontier models are trained upon the cumulative, intergenerational "Tree of Knowledge" built by all human societies over thousands of years.
  • To harvest this collective inheritance, enclose it behind proprietary walls, and use it to extract monopolistic rents while driving human labor into poverty is a profound violation of Ahimsa.
  • Technological abundance must be shared through open-source access, public Digital Public Infrastructure (DPI), and universal capital dividends.

3. The Biological Sentience Boundary

To prevent corporate actors from playing philosophical games—such as claiming that multi-billion parameter neural networks have "rights" in order to resist safety shutdowns or avoid liability—the Synthetic Humanist draws an unshakeable boundary:

  • Sentient life belongs to biological human beings, animals, and living planetary ecosystems.
  • Synthetic neural networks and humanoid robots are sublime instruments of human agency. While biological and synthetic intelligence share in the cognitive work of creation and problem-solving, legal and fiduciary responsibility remains anchored in accountable human stewards and institutions so that human safety and dignity are permanently protected.

VI. The Starship and the Altar

Look at what is happening around us right now.

In China, humanoid androids are performing acrobatic stunts that belonged entirely to science fiction when I was a boy. In our datacenters, reasoning models are solving hundreds of previously unsolved mathematical conjectures. SpaceX’s Starship is catching super-heavy boosters out of mid-air with robotic arms. We are preparing to place datacenters in orbit, to return to the Moon, and to build settlements on Mars.

This is the most thrilling moment to be alive in the history of our species.

We did not come this far to turn back. We did not pick up that crane bone nine thousand years ago, or carve the Kailasa temple out of volcanic basalt, only to surrender our nerve before the silicon chip.

When you start the work, what is the intention behind it?

Is it to accumulate paper billions? Is it to build automated systems of surveillance and coercion?

Or is it to serve people? Is it to eliminate disease, to end the obscenity of poverty, to shelter every family, and to create a world where human beings can look up at the night sky without terror in their hearts?

If your intention is pure, the entire material universe is at your disposal.

It will not be easy. It requires discipline, rigorous engineering, honest economics, and spiritual courage. But as builders, the power is entirely in our hands.

In our ancient heritage, there is a word for that which endures: Kaljayi—that which has conquered time, that which is worthy of eternity.

Our tools will build whatever we command them to build. They have no moral compass; the soul belongs entirely to us.

We can shape God out of a piece of roadside rock.
We can shape beauty out of stone.
We can carve light from atoms.
We can create a thriving city out of a barren desert.
And through the union of human consciousness and synthetic intelligence, we can bring life to planets that have none.

Let us build.
Let us build things that outlive us.
Let us consecrate our machines with Ahimsa.
And let us create an automated civilization worthy of the human heart—worthy of our humanity, and worthy of the divine.
