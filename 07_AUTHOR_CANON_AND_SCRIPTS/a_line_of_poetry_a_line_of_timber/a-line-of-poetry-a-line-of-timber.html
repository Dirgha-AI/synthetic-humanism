I

The Mark of Violence

War Deadens, Streets Harden

War deadens you; street hardens you.

I’ve seen boys beaten to pulp, and could do nothing to help them. I’ve come this close to getting smashed, cut, or shot, and during those darkest moments of rage, considered violence—its violent delights.

Art saved me. Somehow I would end up pouring all that vengefulness and anger, fear and blood, into whatever I was doing at the moment: drawing, journaling, poetry, screenwriting. And find peace. A kind of solace.

The trauma of Partition is the defining fracture of modern Indian history. It is the story of a civilization sliced by bureaucratic ink—first in 1947, and again in 1971—leaving deep civilizational wounds across our borders, our collective psyche, and contiguous cultural landscapes like the Terai-Madhesh borderlands between India and Nepal.

When you grow up with the ambient memory of that division, borders are never abstract political lines; they are living scars etched into the earth. In my early work, I wrestled with these wounds in color and image: my first MS Paint digital artwork, Wounded Boy (2005); Motherland in watercolor and crayon (2006); and The Assassination of Gandhi in ink on paper (2006).

The past is never dead in my illustrated poetry book The Story of India’s Partition, nor in “Shambhala”, my short story about the displacement of a Tibetan family published by Juggernaut in 2016. It is a meditation on faith, poetry, and violence—recurring themes in all my work.

II

The Drones Above,
The Bones Below

Speculative Literature & the Algorithmic State

Yet meditation cannot exist in a vacuum that denies the reality of power. Power is the fundamental physics of civilization. When power is ignored, violence fills the vacuum.

In March 2017, New Myths published my poem “Which species of bird is a drone?” It documented the history of human violence—from prehistoric slaughter to autonomous machines of destruction:

“An obsidian arrowhead sticks out of Nataruk’s skull like the boy emperor’s decayed tooth—a dumb relic of regret, quarantined peace. On prominent display: seared columns of his fabled empire, bleached bones of his peacock throne. There were times when his bush-black hair harbored lightening; when babies weren’t printable like maps or ships; a time when people knew that pulsating roar of intelligence—now hovering, already gone—was the sound of the man eating himself.”

Later that year, I read “Field Notes” at the Partition Museum project at Oxford Bookstore in Delhi—tracing the tolls of identity, borders, and the wounds of 1947 as I got lost in the chaotic streets of Old Delhi searching for the home of Mirza Ghalib. In “Lakhen and Dragonflies” (La.lit, 2016), micro-drone war ensects and border occupations exploded to the surface.

This investigation reached its maturity in The American Emperor Duology (2026), examining what happens when a desperate democracy surrenders its moral agency to CHIMERA—an autonomous AI algorithm that partitions a nation and assumes nuclear command. Two thousand years ago, an anonymous mathematician proposed the Shunya—the zero. The academy scoffed. Yet without the void, there is no calculus, no architecture, and no computing. Today, AI is our new Shunya.

III

Cinema as Total Architecture

From Bergman to the Global Indian Epic

In 2009, I published an essay titled “Teach Cinema to Children”. I wrote: “The man is only half himself, the other half is his expression.”

Cinema is the art of all arts. It is the language that systematizes time, space, light, sound, psychology, and architectural mise-en-scène. When I discovered cinema as a teenager through P2P networks, Satyajit Ray’s Apu Trilogy was my first download; Vittorio De Sica’s The Bicycle Thief was my second. But it was Ingmar Bergman and his legendary partnership with cinematographer Sven Nykvist that transformed my worldview forever. They demonstrated that light itself is a spiritual substance.

For decades, auteur filmmaking remained one of the most expensive and gatekept art forms in human history. Visionary projects were routinely killed because capital and physical production pipelines were monopolized.

With frontier technology and generative spatial tools, that gate is shattered. Today, a single director can orchestrate the complete look, feel, tone, lighting, and mise-en-scène of an entire universe. India has finally matured as an economic and cultural titan. Our audiences are educated, ambitious, and hungry for original mythology executed at global Hollywood scale.

This is the bedrock of our upcoming cinematic adaptation of Shiva and Shakti—staging Mount Kailash against cosmic war. It is not an escape from reality; it is an architectural confrontation with the sublime.

IV

The Sovereign Dialectic

Tipu’s Tiger & Durga’s Vahaan

Why the Tiger? Why The Bagh?

In Indian history, the tiger occupies a fierce dialectic between imperial defiance and sacred transcendence.

In the late eighteenth century, Tipu Sultan of Mysore chose the tiger as the supreme emblem of his sovereign realm. His mechanized tiger mauled the invading British officer; his throne rested upon a golden tiger head; his soldiers wore the tiger-stripe bubri motif; his cannons were cast with roaring tiger muzzles. When asked why he courted total war against the British East India Company, Tipu famously declared:

“It is far better to live like a tiger for a day than to live like a sheep for two hundred years.”

That is the athletic, unyielding defiance of native sovereignty—the refusal to submit to subjugation, the insistence on self-determination at any cost. Yet martial defiance alone can curdle into destruction. It requires a higher spiritual anchor.

In Vedic metaphysics, the tiger is the divine Vahaan (mount) of Goddess Durga—the primordial Shakti. When the combined hosts of heaven were routed by Mahishasura, the gods pooled their concentrated celestial radiance (Tejas) to manifest the Mother Goddess. To Her, the king of the Himalayas gifted the ferocious mountain tiger. Riding this roaring beast into cosmic battle, Durga wields the weapons of all the gods not out of cruelty, but out of absolute, unshakeable Dharma. Her tiger represents controlled, consecrated force—the power that protects the vulnerable, vanquishes darkness, and establishes righteous sovereignty.

The Bagh Collection embodies this exact synthesis: Sacred Power. It rejects the cynical brutality of the modern machine age, and the spineless, disposable fragility of consumer furniture. It stands like Durga's mount—muscular, grounded, alert, and holy.

V

The Chisel and the Verse

The Tectonics of Poetry, Wood, and Stone

People often ask me how a poet, science fiction writer, and filmmaker turns his hand to heavy timber, cast brass, and stone. To me, there is no division. The discipline is identical.

Poetry is not the accumulation of decorative words. Poetry is the rigorous, relentless art of subtraction. You begin with an empty page, a chaotic storm of raw memory, intuition, and rhythm. Then begins the quiet discipline of the chisel: you strike away every false word, every self-indulgent syllable, every ornamental adjective that clouds the central pulse. You plane each line down to its bare, ringing grain. You chisel and strike until nothing more can be removed without the poem collapsing into silence.

The chisel on wood and stone obeys the exact same law.

When I stand before a monumental trunk of hundred-year-old Indian teak, or a raw block of Makrana marble, there is already an architectural vision burning in my mind. The form is already sleeping inside the matter—the athletic tension of a crouching feline leg, the continuous sweep of a catenary arch, the quiet threshold of a sanctum bench. The work of the designer, like the work of the poet, is to strip away the illusion of the extraneous.

With gouge, adze, drawknife, and planer, the artisan strikes away the excess. You listen to the timber’s resistance. You follow the grain when it turns, respect the knots where the branch once fought the storm, and honor the heartwood fissures where the tree dried under the monsoon sun. You do not impose synthetic trickery over its fractures; you carve, you hone, you heal with molten gold. You keep chiseling away until you arrive at the timeless trinity of our ancestors:

SATYAM · SHIVAM · SUNDARAM

“A line of poetry and a line of timber are born from the same chisel. Both are an attempt to carve a clearing of stillness in a noisy, disposable world.”

The Bagh Collection, epigraph
