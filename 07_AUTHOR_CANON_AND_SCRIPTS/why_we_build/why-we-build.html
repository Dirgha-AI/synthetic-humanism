Essay / Art, craft & technology

Why We Build

On beauty, craft, and the machines that inherit our hands.

Salik Shah October 2026 20 min read

IThe First Making

Look around at the spaces we inhabit today.

For thousands of years, people built things meant to outlive them. We carved stone into cliffs. We joined timber so well that wooden temples survived earthquakes for centuries. We laid foundations for generations whose names we would never know.

In the last century, we learned how to build faster than ever before. We laid thousands of miles of rail. We put roofs over billions of heads. That was real, hard-won human progress.

Yet along the way, we made a quiet trade.

We started building for speed, for turnover, and for financial quarters. Beauty stopped being essential. It became an optional luxury—an added cost to be trimmed away.

Today, our machines can build almost anything we can imagine. The question is no longer can we build.

The question is: what are we building for?

Think about why we started making things in the first place.

A stone hand axe makes obvious sense. It chops wood. It cuts meat. It solves an immediate biological problem.

Yet thousands of years before human beings built settled towns or planted crops, we were making things that solved no practical problem at all.

Nine thousand years ago, along the Yellow River, an artisan hollowed out the wing bone of a crane. They carved seven small holes into the marrow.

They blew across that bone, moving their fingers to discover what correct music sounded like.

That bone was not a knife. It could not feed a hungry family.

Before we had permanent roofs over our heads, we were tuning instruments to discover harmony.

If our ancestors only cared about survival, they would have made more axes. But they didn't. They made music. They made meaning.

That truth stayed in my mind on a quiet Sunday morning in May.

I sat down to meditate, but my thoughts refused silence. I was thinking about historical rupture—about the Bengal famine, where millions starved while warehouses and rail networks sat fully intact. It was proof that logistical power without human care is catastrophe.

I could not put what I felt into words. So I opened a prompt and asked an AI model to render something I could not name.

What came back stunned me.

An emaciated holy man, gaunt from fasting, crossing an open grid. Reaching toward a spiral of light. A peacock on one side. A deer on the other.

His ribs were an architectural vault of bone. But his eyes were wide open. Serene. Completely steady.

When I searched the human record, I realized that exact figure had already been carved two thousand years ago in Gandhara, chiseled out of mountain stone.

The ancient carver spent years with a hammer and chisel. The neural network generated its variation in seconds.

Both are instruments. Neither is the author.

The algorithm has no soul. It knows nothing of hunger, of devotion, or of grief. It cannot weep.

The tool rendered the image. The longing came entirely from the human spirit.

That realization made me want to step away from the screen.

I had been working on a digital piece depicting Krishna washing the feet of his poorest childhood friend, Sudama. The master of the universe kneeling on cold stone, holding the dust of his friend's feet with absolute tenderness.

The digital rendering was complete on my monitor. But a file on a hard drive is not enough. It cannot catch the morning light. It has no weight.

So I took the design into the real world.

I went to traditional printmakers and woodcarvers, searching for ways to set the artwork into seasoned timber and brass inlays.

The software helped me balance the proportions. But the software knows nothing of friendship. It knows nothing of the dust on an old friend’s feet, or the sacred silence of care.

The computer helped map the form. But it takes human hands and natural materials to give it a soul.

In our ancient heritage, this understanding was distilled into three words:

Satyam.
Shivam.
Sundaram.

Truth in the structure.
Goodness in the purpose.
Radiant beauty in the form.

Our first architecture was never stone or concrete. It was an ancestor carrying burning embers into a dark cave, sitting in a circle of warmth with others.

We build because we want to share warmth in the dark.

And the question now is whether our newest tools will honor that circle, or leave it behind.

IIThe Material Argument

Long before I ever worked with software or designed furniture, this began in the quiet of dawn.

I grew up in Kathmandu. Before first light, I would climb the ancient stone steps of Swayambhunath, watching the sun break through the white mist across the valley.

That mountain silence stayed in my bones.

Years later, I went looking for that silence again. I spent ten days in complete noble silence at a retreat in the Shivapuri Hills, and in the quiet of Dharamkot in Dharamshala.

Ten days. No phone. No talking. No screens.

When you sit with your own breath for that long, the noise of daily life falls away.

You encounter what our ancestors called Shunya—the fertile stillness.

And you realize something simple:

Beauty is not decoration.

Beauty is what remains when every unnecessary lie has been stripped away.

Now consider how we build today.

Modern buildings can be bad when they are not beautiful.

You can be a harsh critic of urban development, but do not forget: concrete and steel gave shelter to billions of human beings in a single century, and that is an incredible achievement.

And there’s the matter of personal taste. Even if the outside shell of a building is concrete, the inside can be warm, rich, and filled with soul.

Modern manufactured tiles and engineered materials can be durable and strong. That is not the issue.

The issue is how hurried our world became.

We started putting up readymade, copy-pasted cities overnight. We see giant, shining megaprojects like NEOM—bold, radical ideas on billboards that struggle because they forget the human scale. And then we look at a place like Chandigarh: planned with green boulevards, shaded trees, courtyards, and breathing room for ordinary families.

One was built for spectacle. The other was planned for life.

A few years ago, I built my own home.

And when you build a home, you realize something ancient: building shelter is one of the most spiritual acts a human being can undertake.

You cannot escape the feeling of touching the earth.

The heavy grain of old teak wood. The cool weight of a granite floor. The rough texture of sand, stone, and iron.

Our tools and construction methods have changed. But when you lay those materials down, you are touching things that were formed millions of years ago. You are anchoring your life into the history of the planet.

And inside that home, every single object holds a story.

Think about the furniture you grew up with.

Somewhere along the way, modern culture developed an odd sense of shame about hand-me-downs. We were told that inheriting your mother’s dining table or an old wooden desk was outdated. We were told to throw it out and buy a flat-pack replacement every three years.

Fast furniture became just like fast food: cheap, convenient, and completely disposable.

Just because an industry can make something cheap, doesn't mean we have to live with things that hold no soul.

What makes an object sacred is memory.

The small dent in the corner of a wooden table where your child dropped a toy. The brass handle worn smooth by thirty years of your mother's hands.

Those marks are not defects. They are living proof of a life shared together.

Most of us spend ten hours a day trapped behind glowing screens.

Our fingers slide across smooth glass, but we never touch anything real.

Ask yourself honestly: when was the last time you walked barefoot on cool grass? When was the last time your bare feet touched raw stone?

It feels foreign now. It feels rare.

That is why millions of people on YouTube spend hours watching strangers restore rusty, forgotten hand tools, or quietly cut overgrown lawns.

It is deeply cathartic.

We watch it because our hands miss matter. We miss the feeling of taking something broken, cleaning the rust away, and bringing it back to life.

I think often of a craftsman named Jess Elofson. Jess is a potter and a welder in South Dakota, farming the exact piece of prairie his family homesteaded in eighteen seventy-six.

He builds wood-burning stoves by welding heavy scrap excavator buckets from junkyards—turning discarded industrial steel into heating vessels that will outlast his grandchildren.

And he built a wood kiln out of five thousand bricks with his own hands.

Jess taught me a simple lesson in physics: an open wood fire on the ground can never get hotter than fourteen hundred degrees.

Yet inside his kiln, the fire reaches over two thousand three hundred degrees. Not because of better fuel, but because of geometry.

The curve of the arch. The draft of the chimney.

Shape creates the heat.

His grandfather lived through the Great Depression on that farm and told his father: If you want nice things, you better learn to build them yourself, because we don’t have any money.

That isn't just old-fashioned grit. It’s a quiet truth: if an object is going to share your life, you should know how to care for it.

For thousands of years, architecture was an act of devotion.

At the Kailasa temple, ancient builders carved top-down into a volcanic mountain. Two hundred thousand tons of basalt rock, sculpted by hand across generations without a single miscalculation.

Yet stone is not the only way to endure.

I grew up between the plains and the mountains.

Snow-covered peaks look serene on phone screens and desktop wallpapers. But in the real world, danger is always woven into that beauty.

Just this past August, a massive glacier collapsed in the Langtang range. A wall of ice, mud, and water roared down the gorge and struck Betrawati—a historic town painstakingly rebuilt by hand after the 2015 earthquake.

The flood wiped it clean in fifteen minutes.

Centuries of hand-carved wooden shrines and family courtyards dissolved into river silt.

An organic civilization of timber and earth leaves no stone ruins behind.

Our cities have always carried the memory of floods, fire, and human tragedy. What keeps a culture alive is never just stone standing in the dust.

It is the living obligation to rebuild with care.

It is the choice to repair what was broken.

To hold onto the things that carry memory.

And to build spaces worthy of the human heart.

IIIThe Hand and the Machine

Everything so far has been about what we lost.

Now comes the harder question: how we bring it back.

Because right now, we are watching ancient craft and digital intelligence collide. Both moments happened on the exact same soil.

The first took place in Saharanpur.

For centuries, this city has been the woodcarving capital of northern India.

The moment you walk through its alleys, you smell it: the rich, sweet scent of sheesham and teak sawdust hanging in the air. From every doorway, you hear the steady, rhythmic tapping of steel chisels hitting wood.

Someone had given me the name of a master carver. I went searching for him through the narrow maze of Khata Kheri.

I found him sitting on the concrete floor of a tiny, matchbox room.

He was surrounded by curls of shaved wood. He was so completely lost in his craft that when I stepped into the doorway, he didn't even look up.

Behind him, resting in the shadows, were carved wooden lions. They were fierce, muscular, and alive with power.

When he finally paused his chisel to greet me, I felt an immediate wave of embarrassment for interrupting him.

Later that evening, he sent photographs to my phone. Dozens of them: intricate wooden temples, traditional swings, and architectural screens he had carved over a lifetime.

A few doors down, in another workshop, a modern furniture exporter told me their team now uses generative AI to brainstorm design variations for international buyers.

Think about the contrast between those two rooms.

I should be honest: I work digitally.

I am not a traditional woodcarver, and I am not a metalsmith. My daily work lives in software, in 3D geometry, and in creative direction.

And whenever modern technologists interact with traditional craftsmen, you run directly into an old, lingering prejudice.

We inherited a colonial habit that treats English fluency as a test of intelligence.

Because a craftsman in Khata Kheri doesn't speak fluent English, polite society assumes his skills belong to the past. We assume someone typing in an air-conditioned office is more sophisticated than a man holding a gouge on the floor.

That is a complete civilizational mistake.

That carver holds an embodied understanding of geometry, structural tension, and grain that no prompt engineer can fake.

An algorithm can dream up thousands of 3D designs in a minute. But an algorithm only dreams. It knows nothing of the density of teak. It has never felt the bite of a chisel.

To turn a digital dream into something you can touch, you still need the master hand.

We don't need technology to replace the artisan. We need technology to serve the artisan.

The second moment happened at midnight, in the pouring rain.

I was in Mathura for the Janmashtami festival.

Earlier that afternoon, I spent hours hauling heavy wooden stage risers and unrolling carpets alongside local volunteers.

In many cultures, there is an unspoken belief that people who work with their minds shouldn't touch a broom or carry heavy timber. I have never believed that. Physical labor has always been an altar.

By midnight, the monsoon had turned the dirt into thick, clinging mud. It caked onto our boots until every single step weighed ten pounds.

And then, the black storm clouds opened.

Five hundred autonomous aerial drones rose in complete silence into the storm.

Governed entirely by mathematical code, they flew through the rain and painted the radiant wheel of Krishna across the sky. And then, a giant peacock feather turning in golden light.

Standing in that ancient mud, looking up at five hundred synchronized machines, two truths hit me at once:

Technology without the sacred soul is sterile. It builds digital toys that trap us behind screens while our physical neighborhoods decay.

And tradition without technological mastery is fragile. It gathers dust in museums while ordinary families go home to live in disposable boxes.

In Saharanpur, an ancient craft met modern design.

In Mathura, ancient devotion met five hundred autonomous flying machines.

Ancient intention. Frontier computation.

Same soil. Same night.

The machine did what code directed it to do.

The human spirit supplied the aim.

IVThe Consecration

Our ancient tradition has an answer for this. And its philosophy is far more radical than modern engineers realize.

On Vishwakarma Puja, the artisan lays their tools upon the altar.

The stonecutter sets down the chisel. The draftsman puts away the pen. Every machine in the workshop falls silent for an entire day of reverence.

And behind it sits an even sharper ritual: Ayudha Puja—the consecration of implements, and Astra Puja—the consecration of weapons.

On that day, you take every tool of your livelihood: the plough, the loom, the chisel, the laptop.

You clean them. You polish them. You mark them with sandalwood paste and turmeric, the exact same marks you offer to a deity. You place them against the wall, and you are strictly forbidden to work with them.

The tradition even demanded that weapons be placed on that altar.

Why offer reverence to a sword?

Because ancient builders understood something profound: power without purpose is lethal.

A sword can only cut. A chisel can only strike. A neural network can only process numbers.

The tool has no opinion. The entire moral meaning belongs to the hand that aims it.

The ritual never worshipped the iron blade. It sanctified human responsibility.

And remember: in our culture, we revere the sacred river Ganga, but we have never claimed to have built it. Our heritage already knows how to relate to forces larger than ourselves: respect them, consecrate them, but never pretend the instrument can supply an aim of its own.

Consider how our modern instruments actually work.

A human brain runs on about twenty watts of power. That is the energy of a small lightbulb. That tiny flicker of electricity is the entire physical cost of a conscious, loving mind.

Today, training the largest AI superclusters burns more electricity in a single day than your skull uses across an entire lifetime.

We assume computing must always be this brute-force, power-hungry machine. But that is changing.

At the Indian Institute of Technology in Bombay, researchers built a working artificial neuron from a single transistor, running on tiny whispers of electricity that normal chips waste their energy suppressing.

At the Indian Institute of Science in Bangalore, engineers developed a molecular memory film that stores over sixteen thousand distinct states in the space where a standard chip stores just two.

Hardware is finally learning to think the way biology thinks: quiet, efficient, and alert.

Sixty years ago, the astrophysicist Nikolai Kardashev measured civilizations by how much raw energy they could burn.

He was looking at the wrong metric.

The true constraint on human building was never raw power; it was always the precision of our instruments.

Now, those instruments are arriving.

Software models can solve open mathematical problems and calculate complex fluid turbulence in minutes.

Yet when twenty-five of the world’s top mathematicians—including Terence Tao—signed a public declaration on AI, they issued a warning that cuts straight to the heart of our culture.

They warned that treating mathematics like an automated benchmark race risks destroying the fertile soil of human thought. Solving equations is just a mechanism; the true goal of mathematics is human understanding.

Tao compared it to a country dying of thirst while standing in the middle of an ocean.

A machine can churn out correct answers at blinding speed.

But it cannot know which problems are worth solving.

The instrument has no hunger. It has no longing. It is waiting to be aimed.

And now, we are giving these algorithms physical bodies.

Humanoid robots with articulating joints are rolling off production lines.

When the engineering team at Figure retired their first generation of humanoids, they had to protect their proprietary actuators.

So they shipped the retired robots to Finland, trained them in simulation to leap directly into a seventy-five-ton electric arc furnace, and melted them down into white-hot liquid steel.

The metal cooled into solid bars, was shipped back, and machined into commemorative keepsakes.

Notice the pattern across our story:

A block of mountain stone in Gandhara had to be cleaved before an artisan could sculpt the fasting ascetic.

The ancient timber town of Betrawati was wiped clean into river silt in fifteen minutes.

A humanoid robot was dissolved into liquid ore inside an industrial furnace and forged into commemorative steel.

Three collapses. Three scales of time.

The exact same law:

Physical matter transforms and breaks down, but human intention survives the destruction of the vessel.

Our physical reach is extending.

In South Texas, SpaceX launched Starship—a hundred and twenty-four-meter rocket, the largest machine to ever leave the earth, built to make spaceflight industrial and routine.

At the same time, India is preparing the sovereign launch of Gaganyaan under ISRO. Sitting inside that cabin will be Vyommitra—an indigenous half-humanoid robot, engineered to measure the stresses of space before our astronauts fly.

And behind Gaganyaan lies the Bharatiya Antariksh Station, engineered module by module from Sriharikota.

We are not waiting for permission from foreign space manifests. We are building sovereign instruments on our own soil, out of our own materials.

There was never a law of physics that said we could not build across the stars. There was only a missing tool.

That conviction is why software alone became an obsession for me.

I spent weeks working day and night, barely sleeping, pouring myself into building an open-source engineering harness called Dirgha Code.

It crossed twenty thousand downloads from developers all over the world.

And yet, right alongside that milestone came a quiet, sobering realization:

Even that kind of furious human coding may soon belong to the past.

Autonomous AI agents are already learning to tackle long-horizon engineering tasks that used to take human teams weeks of grinding effort. The ground is moving beneath our feet.

And code trapped behind a glass monitor cannot keep the monsoon rain off a roof. It cannot shelter a child. It cannot comfort the human soul.

The true frontier is Bits to Atoms—pulling computational precision off the glass screen and grounding it in physical reality.

Two thousand four hundred years ago, Plato wrote that before any physical object exists, its ideal form exists first in the human mind. Matter is merely an imperfect shadow of that vision.

Today, our screens hold those ideal forms in 3D geometry and spatial mathematics.

I have spent months digitally designing sculptural furniture pieces—capturing the muscular, athletic poise of the tiger with clean modern lines.

For now, that work lives on a hard drive. It is a digital blueprint.

A 3D file on a screen is weightless. But translating intent into physical form is where the real magic happens.

When you take that geometry into century-old teak, the wood will talk back. Every single plank has its own grain, its own moisture, and its own history. Sometimes, seasoned timber splits naturally along its fibers.

In mass manufacturing, a crack is treated like a defect. You throw it away, or you hide it with chemical putty.

We chose the opposite path: drawing on the ancient tradition of kintsugi, we engineered a way to heal those natural splits with molten brass and pure gold foil.

In ancient metallurgy, you never conceal the wound.

You consecrate the fracture with gold.

A human life is not an unbroken sheet of plastic. Every one of us carries scars, fractures, and the memories of storms.

That golden vein running through solid wood is physical proof of a quiet truth:

What was broken can become stronger, and far more beautiful, than it was before.

The tools are already here.

The question remains:

What will we consecrate them to build?

VWhat Should We Build

So what lies ahead of us?

The choice is stark.

On one side lies a sterile, copy-pasted world. Endless concrete cubicles. Totalitarian surveillance. An economic machine that treats human beings like biological gears.

On the other side is a world of grace, beauty, and real abundance.

The physical world around us is not just a design question. It is a moral one.

Consider the speed of what is happening across the world right now.

In China, automated dark factories run through the night with the lights turned off, staffed entirely by industrial robots.

Solar farms are spreading across deserts, producing clean energy faster than anyone predicted.

And we don't have to destroy the earth to build our homes.

Imagine returning to local stone, lime, and timber—sourced cleanly from nearby quarries without backbreaking labor. We can manufacture materials that actively trap carbon instead of pumping smoke into the sky. We can build homes that breathe naturally with the sun and the wind, rooted in their own soil.

Half a century ago, Buckminster Fuller looked at our planet and called it Spaceship Earth.

He said humanity already has the know-how and the energy to feed, clothe, and shelter every living person.

The only thing holding us back is an outdated accounting system—a habit of organizing life around artificial scarcity.

Today, that old scarcity is cracking wide open.

Elon Musk and frontier engineers predict that robotics will take over repetitive factory work, dangerous chores, and manual logistics.

Why should a human being spend forty years doing backbreaking, soul-crushing labor in a windowless warehouse just to earn the right to eat and sleep?

The machine can lift that burden.

Work can become a choice—like tending vegetables in a garden—instead of a cage to survive.

I look at this future with a sense of wonder.

Maybe it sounds like childlike optimism. But it is the exact same excitement that drew me to the golden age of science fiction.

Back then, the future was an open horizon of adventure, curiosity, and boundless possibility.

Then, a heavy darkness took over. The brooding dystopia of Dark City became the only story people knew how to tell.

And something tragic happened:

The very writers and thinkers who were supposed to herald the future became its gatekeepers.

Instead of imagining new worlds, they turned around to attack and cancel anyone who dares to create with new tools like AI.

I never expected the dreamers to become the opposition.

The concerns about this technology are real. I spent years exploring those dangers in my own fiction—in my duology, The American Emperor, through The American Partition and Artificial Extinction.

I know how dark the road can turn.

Yet I refuse to stand on the sidelines, judging and fearmongering.

I work.

Because when you actually do the work, you realize the future is not set in stone.

In nineteen seventy-four, an eighteen-year-old Steve Jobs walked barefoot across India with a backpack.

He spent months meditating in the foothills of the Himalayas, stripping away everything he had been taught.

Years later, he shared a realization that never left me:

Look at everything around you that you call life. It was made up by people who were no smarter than you.

The world is not a fixed museum that you simply inhabit.

The world is malleable. You can influence it. You can change it. You can build new things that other people can live inside.

The universe is waiting to be shaped.

In our ancient heritage, there is a word for this:

Kaljayi.

That which has conquered time. That which is worthy of eternity.

Our ancestors proved that civilization endures when each generation passes down the obligation to build with care.

Why do we build?

We build because a million and a half years ago, an ancestor carried fire into a dark cave so a family could sit in a circle of warmth.

We build because twenty-three thousand years ago, someone laid a woven mat in the dirt and ringed it with stones so another human being could sleep in peace.

Satyam.
Shivam.
Sundaram.

Truth in the structure.
Goodness in the purpose.
Radiant beauty in the form.

Our tools will build whatever we command them to build. The machine has no moral compass. The aim belongs entirely to us.

We do not build to conquer time.

We build so that whatever we touch becomes worthy of time.

So let us build things that outlast us.

Let us build a world worthy of our humanity.

A world worthy of the divine.
